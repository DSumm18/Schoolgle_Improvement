# Gunpowder Plot: education and narrative specification

Reviewed 7 September 2026 for Schoolgle Learning Worlds. Audience: teacher-selected ages approximately 6–9, with narrated/short-text support and a deeper evidence discussion for older pupils. Age range is a design recommendation, not an independently validated reading or safety rating. This document specifies proposed content; implementation and browser QA are separate.

## Core idea

**The Midnight Letter — a Gunpowder Plot investigation.** The player is a present-day history explorer visiting a stylised reconstruction. Their job is to discover what happened, connect clues and create an accurate remembrance display. The reward is understanding the story; the child does not become a plotter, handle explosives or rewrite the historical outcome.

Suggested opening, original game narration:

> A warning with no name. A search in the dark. A story remembered for more than four hundred years. Follow the lanterns through our reconstruction of London in 1605. Can you find the evidence and put the story together?

## Verified facts and ready-to-use short narration

| Topic | Child-facing wording | Source and precision |
|---|---|---|
| Setting and intended attack | “In London in 1605, a small group planned to blow up the House of Lords while King James I and others were inside for the opening of Parliament. The plan failed.” | [UK Parliament: plot FAQ](https://www.parliament.uk/about/faqs/house-of-commons-faqs/gunpowder-plot/). Planned date: 5 November 1605. This was an intended lethal attack, not a prank or firework party. |
| People | “Robert Catesby led the plot. Guy Fawkes was one of the plotters. He was found with the hidden gunpowder.” | [UK Parliament: the plot](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/parliamentaryauthority/the-gunpowder-plot-of-1605/overview/the-plot-and-its-discovery/conspiracy-and-deception/). Do not make Fawkes the sole planner or leader. |
| Religious context | “Catholics faced harsh restrictions because of their religion. A small group chose a violent plot. They did not speak for all Catholics. People should not be blamed for sharing someone else's religion.” | Historical context: [The National Archives education pack, background](https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/education/gunpowder.pdf). The final sentence is an explicit present-day teaching principle. Explain Catholic and Protestant as Christian groups; avoid implying that everyone in either group thought alike. |
| Warning | “On 26 October 1605, Lord Monteagle received a letter warning him to stay away from Parliament. He passed it to the King's advisers.” | [UK Parliament: key dates](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/parliamentaryauthority/the-gunpowder-plot-of-1605/keydates/october-1605--1606/). The letter's date of receipt is not its printed date: the surviving letter is undated. |
| Author uncertainty | “We do not know for certain who wrote the warning. A historian can say ‘we do not know’ when the evidence cannot tell us.” | [The National Archives: Monteagle Letter](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/stories/the-monteagle-letter/). Francis Tresham has been suspected; do not mark him as the known author. |
| Discovery | “During searches on the night of 4–5 November 1605, officials found Guy Fawkes and the gunpowder beneath the House of Lords. The planned explosion did not happen.” | [UK Parliament: key dates](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/parliamentaryauthority/the-gunpowder-plot-of-1605/keydates/october-1605--1606/), [discovery and flight](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/parliamentaryauthority/the-gunpowder-plot-of-1605/overview/the-plot-and-its-discovery/discovery-and-flight1/). “Night of 4–5 November” avoids false precision across accounts using evening, midnight or early 5 November. |
| Remembrance | “Bonfire Night remembers the failure of the plot. People first marked the King's escape. Traditions have changed over time.” | [UK Parliament: remembrance FAQ](https://www.parliament.uk/about/faqs/house-of-commons-faqs/gunpowder-plot/), [London Museum](https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/london-stories/guy-fawkes-gunpowder-plot/). Do not suggest the Palace exploded or that every person celebrates today. |

Keep detailed torture, execution, weapons and kidnapping accounts out of the young-child core path. Teachers may contextualise consequences using suitable resources. Do not conceal that the plot threatened lives; explain it plainly without graphic spectacle.

## Five purposeful missions

Each mission needs an action, a thinking requirement, specific feedback and a recorded response before its one-time reward. Reading help and control help are access choices, not wrong answers. The following are original activity designs, not museum worksheets.

| Mission and scene | What the child does | Objective and completion evidence | Feedback / deeper option |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. The people behind the story** — lantern-lit riverside map | Inspect three illustrated person cards. Place Catesby at “led the plot”, Fawkes at “found with the gunpowder”, and James I at “king who was a target”. Use tap-select/tap-place or keyboard alternatives. | Distinguish historical roles. Save every submitted mapping, not just final correctness. Require all three correct before collecting the story seal. | If Catesby/Fawkes are reversed, compare their short role descriptions. Then ask which statement is accurate: Fawkes was one member of a group. |
| **2. The unsigned warning** — writing room | Open an original game paraphrase of the warning. Select the words showing what Monteagle was told to do; attach an “author uncertain” tag. | Retrieve a message's meaning and recognise an evidence limit. Save selected phrase, author response, read-aloud/hint context. Both must be correct. | Correct evidence is the instruction to avoid Parliament. A missing signature does not let us identify the author. Older children distinguish “what the letter says” from “what we infer”. |
| **3. What can a clue tell us?** — reconstructed search display | Use a lantern highlight or an accessible inspect button on a letter, a labelled barrel display and a modern celebration poster. Sort them into “evidence about the warning”, “reconstruction of the hidden material”, and “later remembrance”. Explain why a modern poster cannot tell us exactly what the room looked like in 1605. | Distinguish original-source information, a reconstruction and later representation. Save inspected items and all submitted source/claim matches separately. Completion requires the explanation. | Finding every glowing item is navigation, not learning. Never call an original 3D barrel “a surviving barrel from the plot”. Link the reconstructed display to the historical record. |
| **4. The night in order** — courtyard story trail | Arrange four story panels: warning received, search finds Fawkes, planned attack prevented, later annual remembrance. Place the first two against 26 October and the night of 4–5 November; show the intended 5 November attack as an event that did **not** happen. | Sequence and explain a simple cause/consequence link. Save submitted order and reason: searches uncovered the danger before the planned attack. | Use “before”, “after”, “because”. Do not place a false “Parliament explodes” card as a real event. Older children discuss how several actions, not a magical letter alone, interrupted the plot. |
| **5. Remember the evidence** — present-day remembrance gallery | Build a final display from three accurate captions: Catesby and Fawkes had different roles; the plot failed; the warning's author remains uncertain. Add a short spoken-to-an-adult or typed explanation of what Bonfire Night remembers. | Retrieve key facts and communicate a supported account. Save selected captions; pupil-authored text, if offered, is unscored until an adult reviews it. | Offer a later check with a different wording/order. Do not treat selecting a prepared caption as independent writing or oral-language assessment. |

For the smallest implementation, combine missions 1 and 3 into one evidence room, keeping role matching and source reasoning distinct in the record. Avoid bolting maths onto gunpowder barrels merely to add a subject badge. History and English comprehension supply a coherent first release; chronological ordering is not by itself a maths attainment assessment.

### Original adapted warning for mission 2

Display an always-visible label: **“A short retelling of the warning's meaning. These are our words, not the original letter.”**

> Find a reason not to attend Parliament. Stay away so that you can be safe. Danger is expected there, although things may look quiet.

Suggested prompt: “Which part tells Monteagle what to do?” Correct selection: the first sentence. Follow-up: “Do we know who wrote the original warning?” Answer: not for certain. Do not add a signature, a cipher, the plotters' names or an explicit quantity of gunpowder to the letter; those are not in this adapted passage. The real document can be linked for grown-ups as catalogue reference **SP 14/216/2**. [National Archives education pack, source 1](https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/education/gunpowder.pdf).

## Curriculum mapping and sensible claims

England's **KS1 history** programme includes nationally/globally significant events beyond living memory, including events remembered through festivals or anniversaries. It also requires chronological awareness, questions answered using stories/sources and understanding representations of the past. Bonfire Night is a reasonable school-selected example; the DfE does not mandate this particular topic or a fixed year. This five-mission adventure contributes to those objectives, not the whole programme. Older pupils can revisit it with stronger source reasoning. [DfE history programme, KS1](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-history-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-history-programmes-of-study).

English contributions are listening/reading comprehension, vocabulary and explaining reasons. Oral retelling needs an adult observation; reading with narration does not establish independent decoding. The optional written display label creates a writing opportunity, but the game must not automatically mark historical correctness as writing quality. [DfE English programme](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-english-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-english-programmes-of-study).

Use the shared evidence approach from [the education audit](EDUCATION-AUDIT-2026.md): game/item version, objective, actual response, first/retry attempt, support before that attempt, timestamp and later-check interval. Report “correct after a hint” rather than “mastered Gunpowder Plot”. Instruction → action → feedback → fresh retrieval is a design informed by EEF principles; the game is not EEF evaluated or endorsed.

## Rhyme and remembrance

Use only this traditional opening:

> Remember, remember, the fifth of November

Label it **“Opening of a traditional remembrance rhyme.”** London Museum records the familiar rhyme in its account of later traditions. It helps remember the date; it is not eyewitness evidence, a quotation from Fawkes or proof of the letter's author. Do not invent a precise author or creation date. Do not use modern song recordings/arrangements or append sectarian verses. An original spoken delivery of the brief opening is enough. [London Museum: Guy Fawkes and the Gunpowder Plot](https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/london-stories/guy-fawkes-gunpowder-plot/).

## Visual and narrative accuracy checklist

- **1605 is early Stuart, not Tudor England.** Medieval buildings still existed. Describe the stylised setting as London in 1605; do not present a single accurate street plan unless researched.
- **No modern Big Ben/Elizabeth Tower in 1605.** Its clock and bell began operating in 1859. A separate labelled present-day gallery may show it. [Parliament's tower timeline](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/building/palace/big-ben/building-clock-tower/key-dates-/).
- Parliament did not burn down in the plot. Do not confuse this with the Great Fire of London or the later fire at Parliament.
- Fawkes was not alone; Catesby led. Neither Catholic nor Protestant is shorthand for “villain”. Do not give an entire religious community responsibility for the plot.
- The warning author remains uncertain. Do not reward a confidently invented culprit.
- Dates should separate receipt of the warning, the overnight discovery and the **intended** attack. The 26 October date belongs to receipt, not a visible date written on the real letter.
- Letter art, character dialogue, routes, inspection mechanics and seals are original game inventions. Identify facsimiles, paraphrases and reconstructions clearly.
- Use sealed display props, lamplight, footsteps, river reflections and a slowly revealed investigation board for mystery. The child should neither assemble/fire explosives nor be rewarded for harming a character.
- Avoid countdown pressure, jump scares, flashing fireworks or mandatory sound. Offer a brighter setting; an unhurried lantern effect with a plain inspect-button alternative is enough.

## Verification before calling the educational flow complete

Test every mission with a wrong response first. Confirm each required explanation and evidence step gates completion, collected seals award once, and replay/reload retains actual attempts without merging with Nile Quest. Inspect the grown-up record against the exact responses used. Verify that the unknown-author answer, no-explosion outcome and role distinctions survive simplification and narration. Have a primary teacher check wording and a child explain what happened without reopening the captions. Browser success establishes functional behaviour; comprehension and engagement still require that human check.
