# Nile Quest: teaching and pilot guide

Content checked 6 September 2026. Target for first teacher review: Years 3–4 in England, with adult support as needed and consolidation for older primary pupils. The 7–11 landing-page age range describes the intended audience, not differentiated coverage of every year. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland need their own mappings.

## The curriculum boundary

England's KS2 history programme offers Ancient Egypt as one option for a depth study within the earliest civilisations requirement. It does not prescribe a national Egypt lesson sequence or a particular year group. Nile Quest introduces selected knowledge and enquiry; it does not fulfil a complete depth study or the required overview of early civilisations by itself. [DfE history programme](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-history-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-history-programmes-of-study).

The maths model links Year 2 concrete/pictorial sharing foundations to Year 3 division and the three-times table. It does not assess every division method or written calculation. [DfE mathematics programme](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study).

The shadow activity relates to Year 3 light and lower KS2 scientific enquiry. It is a simplified simulation: follow it with a real torch, opaque object and screen, changing one thing at a time and discussing observed patterns. It is not a replacement for practical science. [DfE science programme](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-science-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-science-programmes-of-study).

Reading a short message and selecting evidence supports lower KS2 comprehension and discussion. Listening provides access to the content, but is not evidence of independent decoding. The game does not assess handwriting, spelling, composition or phonics. [DfE English programme](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-english-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-english-programmes-of-study).

## Evidence-informed design, not an efficacy claim

Teach a small idea, let the child manipulate or apply it, provide feedback and return to it in a new context. The cargo task offers smaller numbers after a difficulty, then returns to the original calculation so the problem is not simply abandoned. A later check can provide a useful discussion prompt; the current three questions are not a validated assessment.

EEF guidance stresses choosing technology to improve teaching, practice and feedback. That supports the rationale for this design; it does not demonstrate that this particular game improves attainment, constitute endorsement or justify a months-of-progress marketing claim. [EEF digital technology guidance](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/digital), [EEF feedback guidance](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/feedback).

Do not describe the prototype as White Rose approved/aligned to a specific small step. The sharing representation is original; a future scheme mapping needs the exact scheme version, relevant step, teacher confirmation and any reuse permissions.

- [Ashmolean Ancient Egypt learning resources](https://www.ashmolean.org/learning-resource-ancient-egypt): classroom ideas and object-based learning.
- [British Museum Tutankhamun trail](https://www.britishmuseum.org/visit/object-trails/tutankhamun-ancient-and-modern-perspectives): context for the king and his tomb.
- [Griffith Institute: Howard Carter](https://tutankhamun.griffith.ox.ac.uk/people/howard-carter) and [Egyptian workforce](https://tutankhamun.griffith.ox.ac.uk/stories/egyptian-workforce): discovery and excavation context, including the contribution of Egyptian workers.

These are references, not blanket permission to republish museum lesson packs, pictures or commercial scheme materials. All game messages, puzzles and object models were created for this prototype. The stylised landscape, light-lock, scarab replica and contemporary explorer are explicitly fictional. No claim is made that the replica was found in Tutankhamun's tomb. Teach beliefs as beliefs and distinguish archaeology from treasure-taking.

## Help children get comfortable

The sphinx is an original stylised sculpture, inspired by the human-headed lion form represented in [the Met's sphinx collection](https://www.metmuseum.org/de/art/collection/search/544442). Painted bands and stone decoration take visual direction from surviving painted objects, including [this British Museum tomb painting](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA37976). The game does not reproduce either museum object. Its repeated decorative signs are **not a translated inscription** or a hieroglyph-reading lesson; use the [British Museum hieroglyph resources](https://www.britishmuseum.org/hieroglyphs-resources) for that teaching. The map still combines different places and periods and is not a reconstruction of one historical site.

**Settings → World lighting** switches between Golden evening and Bright daylight. Let the child choose the more comfortable view. Reduced motion still freezes environmental movement; the new lighting does not introduce flashing effects.

Before starting, choose **Your explorer** for Leo or Maya and a skin tone. All options have the same abilities. Choices are stored only in this browser and can be changed from Settings without affecting discoveries. The game asks for an avatar, not the child's gender.

First-use instructions include optional read-aloud audio. Use **Controls** to revisit them. On tablets, place one thumb on the labelled Move pad and slide gently; lift to stop. The other finger can swipe the world to look. **Look ahead** resets the view, **Run** toggles speed, and **Guide me there** follows a trail automatically. On computers, arrows or WASD walk, Shift runs and E opens a nearby discovery. Opening a panel pauses movement. Losing window focus clears held input and stops travel.

Ask the child to demonstrate moving, stopping, opening a discovery and finding help before judging the learning activities. In short landscape windows, the mission card shows the discovery title and guide button; full teaching instructions appear at the destination. Larger text and narration remain available.

## Suggested teacher-led use

### New studio workflow

Choose **For grown-ups → Plan a guided lesson → Start Teach together**. The game keeps existing progress and enables automatic travel. During play, the studio is accessible from the field journal. All seven core activity sequences remain required; the optional Sphinx challenge unlocks after the harvest boats and cannot replace a chapter.

The Sphinx task asks for 15 shared between three trays: construct [5,5,5], enter 5 for 15 ÷ 3, and select an explanation connecting equal groups and the total. Each submitted stage is recorded separately, including incorrect attempts and support. The model is part of the guided practice; a selected explanation is not equivalent to a spontaneous spoken/written explanation. Completion earns 15 bonus gems once, including supported completion. Follow it with an unfamiliar sharing task away from the game.

**Open this browser's learning record** shows actual submitted choices, quantities, equations, timeline order and support, with timestamped attempts and suggested adult probes. Print or export it for review. New records have a content version; older records are labelled unversioned and missing responses are not reconstructed. Retention is bounded to the latest 200 activity attempts and up to 100 return responses per day across 60 recorded days. These are local guided-practice observations, not all-time pupil totals or statutory assessment.

**Preview a school dashboard** is deliberately fictional data demonstrating a possible teacher workflow. It never joins the local player's record or export. Actual school accounts, verification, assignments and server-side pupil records remain future work. The local URL is not a publicly shareable game address.

### Current evidence and curriculum direction

The [EEF 2025 EdTech review](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/effectiveness-of-edtech-reflections-from-new-review) reinforces implementation, accessibility and established teaching practices; it does not validate this product or establish that gamification alone improves learning. [EEF metacognition guidance, second edition](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/metacognition%20) informs task-specific planning, checking and explanation. The [government's curriculum-review response](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/curriculum-and-assessment-review-final-report-government-response) plans revised curriculum publication in spring 2027 and first teaching in September 2028. Those plans are distinct from current England objectives. The [education audit](EDUCATION-AUDIT-2026.md) provides the detailed coverage, limitations and source checks.

### A suggested first session

1. Before play, locate Egypt and the Nile on a real map and establish what children already know. Explain that the game map combines locations and periods.
2. Play in two short blocks with a break, allowing time for discussion rather than requiring a fixed completion time. Let pupils choose narration, larger text and guided travel.
3. Pause at the baskets: ask the child to explain the method with physical counters. Record whether they needed modelling or prompts.
4. Follow the light activity with a practical investigation. Ask why one changed variable helps comparison.
5. After the museum, use an unfamiliar example outside the game. Return around one week later with parallel questions and record the interval. Avoid rehearsing identical answer positions.

The practice record reports attempts, cumulative support, named objectives and bounded observations of controls used. A sharing button records a sharing action; it does not establish the child’s mental calculation strategy. The cargo and shadow discoveries now require a correct explanation after manipulation, with feedback and another attempt if needed. Read-aloud is recorded as an access choice.

The practice record reports attempts and support. One correct selection, repeated guessing or a completed badge cannot establish secure learning. Interpret it alongside conversation and independent tasks.

## Accessibility and pilot checks

Every narrated mission introduction is also written. Meaning is carried by labels, shapes and text, not colour alone; no task depends on hearing a chime. Keyboard buttons replace timeline dragging and guided brushing replaces fine pointer work. Larger text, automatic travel, untimed play, reduced environmental movement and lighter graphics are available. None of these settings identifies or diagnoses a need.

Remaining validation includes actual children with varied vision, hearing, reading, attention and motor needs; screen readers and switch access; physical iPads and low-cost school laptops; motion comfort; text comprehension; and teacher judgement about historical simplifications. Start with supervised usability, then a small learning pilot with baseline, immediate and delayed questions outside the game. Keep enjoyment, navigation, learning and willingness to pay as separate outcomes.

## Future coverage, deliberately not claimed yet

Broader coverage could add society and roles, religious change and named deities, more authentic sources with competing interpretations, Egyptian achievements, links to other early civilisations, extended writing and additional maths representations. Each needs its own objective and meaningful task. Adding a subject label to a scene is not equivalent to teaching it.

Accounts, parent access and paid school reporting are a separate workstream. Use verified school invitations and explicit authorised linking rather than exposing records based on a typed school name. Confirm the controller/processor arrangements, lawful basis and Children's Code obligations before building real pupil sharing. This local prototype supplies no legal approval for a public child-facing service.
