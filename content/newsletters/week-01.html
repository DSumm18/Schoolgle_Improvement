Ofsted's report cards — what the first wave tells us

The new inspection framework went live in November. The headline grading system is gone. In its place: a five-point scale across six evaluation areas.

The six areas are:

  • Inclusion
  • Curriculum and teaching
  • Achievement
  • Attendance and behaviour
  • Personal development and wellbeing
  • Leadership and governance

Safeguarding stays separate. Met or not met.

Early data from the first wave: roughly 1 in 7 schools are receiving the top "Exceptional" grade. Most land on "Good standard" or "Needs attention."

The marketing problem nobody's talking about

The days of leading with "We're Outstanding" in your prospectus, recruitment ads, and parent communications are over. If you've built your brand around a single Ofsted word, you need a new narrative — and you need it before your next open evening.

Source: Ofsted first wave inspection data, November 2025

Ofsted's inspection software: still broken

Inspectors have been told to follow contingency arrangements from January after continued glitches with the inspection notes software. If Ofsted's own systems aren't reliable, your evidence trail becomes critical.

So what? Six separate grades mean six separate conversations with parents. One weak area is now visible, not hidden behind a single "Good."

Do this next

Snow closures: the decision framework

Met Office cold weather alerts issued for much of England. A clear framework:

  1. By 6:00am — Check roads, staff availability, heating. Can the majority of staff get in safely?
  2. By 6:30am — Decide. Communicate via text system, website, social media, local radio.
  3. Document everything — Record the factors. You need contemporaneous evidence if challenged.
  4. Partial opening — Some year groups if staffing limited. Key worker and vulnerable pupils prioritised.
Do this next
  • Test your parent communication system this week. Better to find out it doesn't work now than at 6:30am in a blizzard. (15 mins)

The question leaders should ask out loud

Should every school have a "snow day" remote learning plan ready to deploy?

Snow closures cost approximately 10 million pupil-days per year across England. COVID showed most schools can deliver some form of remote education. Yet when snow closes a school in 2026, most pupils simply lose a day.

Is the technology and expectation now in place to make snow closures a thing of the past — or is a day off when it snows one of the few genuine joys of childhood?
