Finance

The pay reality: 2.7% headroom, 6.5% ambition

The DfE's own numbers show schools can afford less than half the pay increase the government is recommending. Here's what it means for your budget.

What the DfE actually said

In its annual school costs technical note, the department forecasts schools will have around £1 billion of "headroom" over the next two years. That sounds generous until you do the division: every 1 percentage point pay increase costs schools £330 million nationally.

So £1 billion ÷ £330 million = roughly 3 percentage points. Spread across two years, that's the 2.7% figure. And only £250 million of that headroom falls in 2026-27 — making next year "more challenging" than 2027-28.

The gap

The DfE has recommended 6.5% over three years to the School Teachers' Review Body. Assuming 2.7% is absorbed in years one and two, that leaves 3.9% needed in year three — with no confirmed funding source yet. The DfE's own words: schools will need to "realise and sustain better value from existing spending."

The NEU's Daniel Kebede was blunt: "After 15 years of austerity, there are no easy savings to be made. To call this a 'manageable ask' is not only ludicrous but insulting."

What this means for your budget

ScenarioCost impact (avg primary, 30 teachers)
1% pay rise (all staff)~£33,000 additional cost
2.7% over 2 years~£89,000 additional cost
6.5% over 3 years~£215,000 additional cost

Add to this the SEND provision cost increase the DfE estimates at £880 million nationally over two years. Over £4 billion has been promised for inclusion — but the DfE explicitly excluded this from its affordability calculations.

Radar

Complaints: the new digital platform

The White Paper announces a "digital, accessible solution" for parental complaints. More than 5 million complaints were made last year. Headteachers report sleepless nights. AI-generated complaints are rising. The new platform aims to stop parents escalating through Ofsted, the school, and the DfE simultaneously.

But legal experts warn the "minimum expectations" framework may be advisory rather than compulsory, with no enforcement mechanism when parents don't follow the process.

Do this next
  • Review your complaints policy — is it clear, accessible, and published on your website? (20 mins)
  • Log complaint volumes this term. You'll want baseline data when the digital platform arrives. (15 mins)

Mission North East and Mission Coastal

Two place-based improvement programmes modelled on the London Challenge. Target: entrenched disadvantage and outcomes for white working-class children. Funding amounts and delivery leadership remain unconfirmed. Schools North East warns it "risks becoming symbolic rather than structural" without concrete resources.

Do this next
  • If you're in the North East or a coastal community, register interest with your local Schools Alliance or TSA. Early participants will likely shape the programme. (10 mins)

6,500 teachers: the delivery plan

The government's plan to grow the workforce is published. Key moves: anonymised ITT applications to reduce ethnic disparities, doubled maternity pay (4 weeks → 8), and a new CPD entitlement focused on SEND and leadership. The workforce grew by 2,346 against the target baseline. But experts warn retention, not recruitment, is the real challenge — and the physics teacher shortage alone stands at 3,500.

Do this next
  • Audit your staff retention: who is at risk of leaving? What would keep them? Have those conversations before Easter. (45 mins)

The question leaders should ask out loud

If the DfE's own analysis shows schools can afford 2.7% but needs 6.5% — who pays the difference?

The answer, buried in the technical note, is you. "Schools will need to realise and sustain better value from existing spending." After 15 years of efficiency savings, that's not a strategy — it's a prayer. The real question isn't whether schools can find savings. It's whether the savings they find come from the things that matter most: teaching assistants, pastoral support, curriculum breadth, or the mental health provision that's already stretched to breaking point.

When the pay award lands, will your governors understand the trade-offs? Because that conversation is coming. Better to have it now than in July.
