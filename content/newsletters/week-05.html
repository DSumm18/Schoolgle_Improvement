AI & Tech

Bett 2026: what actually mattered

Expanded floor, AI everywhere, but the real theme was the gap between what's possible and what's affordable.

Bett 2026 at ExCeL London. Expanded exhibition floor, new community villages, Tech User Labs for the first time. The dominant theme: AI. Specifically, the gap between what AI can do in theory and what schools can do in practice.

Several keynotes acknowledged the reality — inconsistent broadband, ageing hardware, staff with no AI training. The sessions that drew standing-room crowds? Practical implementation: AI policy development, data protection implications, AI for reducing teacher workload.

Three tool categories worth watching

  1. AI-assisted marking and feedback — Platforms providing initial feedback on student writing for teacher review. Doesn't replace judgement — reduces time to first draft.
  2. AI for SEND identification — Tools analysing classroom data to flag pupils needing additional support. Early identification is where the system consistently fails.
  3. AI-powered MIS analytics — MIS providers surfacing patterns in attendance, behaviour, achievement. Proactive, not reactive. Only works if underlying data is clean.

The caveat with all three: Data protection. Any AI tool processing pupil data must have a Data Processing Agreement, comply with UK GDPR, and not store or train on pupil data without consent.

Source: Bett 2026 programme; DfE generative AI guidance, August 2025

Do this next
  • If staff attended Bett, schedule a 30-minute debrief. What's worth piloting? (30 mins)
  • One or two targeted pilots with clear success criteria. Not a bag of brochures.

Breakfast clubs: the numbers behind the policy

The government's ambition for universal breakfast provision raises an obvious question: who pays? The National School Breakfast Programme provides some funding, but it doesn't cover full costs for most schools. Whether you're already running a breakfast club or considering starting one, the financial model matters.

Average costs range from £1.50–£3.00 per pupil per day depending on staffing ratios, food costs, and whether you use external providers. At scale, the margins are thin.

AI policy: the gap between guidance and practice

DfE guidance recommends policies. Most schools still don't have one. Three risks of the gap.

The DfE's guidance on generative AI in education (updated August 2025) recommends — but doesn't mandate — that schools develop AI use policies, conduct risk assessments, use enterprise-grade tools with data processing agreements, and communicate with parents.

Deliberately non-prescriptive. No approved tools or banned platforms. In practice: some schools have comprehensive policies, others have nothing.

Three reasons the gap matters

  1. Data protection — Free-tier AI tools may process pupil data outside the UK. No policy = no guardrail.
  2. Academic integrity — Pupils using AI for homework is happening now. No school position = compromised assessment.
  3. Inspection readiness — Ofsted evaluates technology use. "We don't have a policy" is not defensible.

  • Cover: which tools approved for staff, what data can/cannot be entered, expectations for pupils.

Workforce retention: the numbers behind the headlines

40,000 teachers left in 2024-25. Secondary hardest hit. January letters, February submissions.

The School Workforce Census (late 2025): approximately 40,000 teachers left the profession in 2024-25. Secondary schools hit hardest. Most common reasons: workload, pay, lack of professional autonomy.

Schools with strong retention share common features: manageable workload expectations, genuine CPD opportunities, and leaders who listen.

Source: DfE School Workforce Census, 2024-25

Do this next
  • Informal retention check: are there staff showing signs of burnout or disengagement? (ongoing)
  • When did you last ask your teachers what would improve their job? (30 mins)
  • January is when letters get written. February half-term is when they get submitted. Act now.
